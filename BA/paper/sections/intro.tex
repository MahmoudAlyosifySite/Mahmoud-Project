\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Zero-shot voice cloning can now render a few seconds of someone's speech as new sentences in their voice, in dozens of
languages \citep{chen2025valle,ju2024naturalspeech3,chen2025f5tts,du2024cosyvoice2,zhou2026voxcpm2}. For Arabic, ``supported''
usually means Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), the written standard. Very few people speak it at home: everyday
speech in Egypt is Egyptian Arabic, with its own sounds, words and grammar. A system that reads an Egyptian
sentence with MSA or Levantine pronunciation produces every word and still sounds wrong to an Egyptian listener.
Numbers, prices and times have the same problem: \emph{7:30} is \ar{sab3a-wnoss} (``seven and a half'') in
Cairo, and the MSA reading is heard as foreign.

Open models are usually compared on intelligibility, the word or character error rate (WER, CER) of an automatic
speech recogniser run on the output, plus speaker similarity and a predicted quality score. This paper starts from
an observation made while choosing a base model for an Egyptian voice-cloning system: \textbf{for Egyptian Arabic,
none of these measures sees the dialect, and each of them, used to choose a base model, picks the wrong one.} Among
the open models whose weights allow a commercial product, \cbthree has the lowest Egyptian CER (\R{bk:cb-v3:cer}),
yet a dialect classifier labels only \R{bk:cb-v3:c1pct} of its Egyptian sentences Egyptian, against
\R{cal:lahgtna:c1pct} for real colloquial speech. \vox is labelled Egyptian \R{bk:voxcpm2:c1pct} of the time, but over
three seeds its CER and speaker similarity cannot be distinguished from \cbthree's, and its predicted quality is lower:
nothing in the standard metrics favours it. When texts and voices are resampled, selecting the base by lowest CER, by
highest similarity or by highest predicted quality lands on a model below the dialect bar in
\R{sel:eligible:cer:fail1}, \R{sel:eligible:ecapa:fail1} and \R{sel:eligible:dnsmos:fail1} of resamples. Our own
selection rule, committed before any model ran, ranked by CER and chose \cbthree; we amended it, with a dated record of the
reason, before building on the result (\cref{sec:protocol}).

We then build \BA on the model that a dialect-aware rule selects, without training: an Egyptian number and
symbol normaliser, a loanword policy for code-switched text, automatic reference transcription, reference
screening, and long-text chunking with checks. On held-out sentences over three seeds, \BA lowers Egyptian CER from
\R{s3:voxcpm2:egy_main:cer_best} to \R{s3:ba:egy_main:cer_best} and raises speaker similarity from
\R{s3:voxcpm2:egy_main:sim_ecapa} to \R{s3:ba:egy_main:sim_ecapa} relative to the same model used directly. Ablations
trace almost all of this measurable gain to the automatic reference transcript; the Egyptian number normaliser leaves
CER unchanged, because a recogniser writes an Egyptian and an MSA reading of a number as the same digits, a second,
small instance of the same blind spot. Every output carries an inaudible watermark, and the demo will not run without
a consent statement.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The dissociation, stated as a selection failure.} On seven open voice-cloning models, Egyptian CER,
  speaker similarity and predicted quality are blind to the dialect, and selecting by any of them picks a base that
  fails a dialect bar calibrated on real colloquial speech. We quantify this with a bootstrap over texts and voices,
  two dialect classifiers trained on different data, three seeds, and a control for the dialect of the reference
  voice; native listeners confirm it, and two phonetic markers show what the low-CER models lose: \cbthree pronounces
  the Egyptian jiim as [g] in \R{df:cb-v3:jiim} of cases, against \R{df:lahgtna_eg:jiim} in real colloquial speech
  (\cref{sec:dissociation}).
  \item \textbf{A dialect-aware evaluation protocol} for dialectal voice cloning, with gates committed to version control
  before any model ran, every metric calibrated on real speech, and a dated record of the one gate
  amendment (\cref{sec:protocol}).
  \item \textbf{A licence-provenance audit} of the open Egyptian-capable systems, which shows that most cannot be
  built on commercially, including one whose permissive label hides a non-commercial parent (\cref{sec:systems}).
  \item \textbf{\BA, an inference-time engine for Egyptian Arabic} on unmodified \vox weights, with held-out
  results over three seeds, ablations of each component and a long-form test (\cref{sec:engine,sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{A consent-first release}: model, code and demo are public, uploads are not stored, and the
  watermark's robustness to \R{wm:att:n} kinds of audio processing is measured (\cref{sec:release}).
\end{enumerate}
\BA is not a new acoustic model, and we do not claim one: the paper's evidence concerns how such systems should be
chosen and evaluated for a dialect, and how far an engine around an unchanged model can go. A blind test with
\R{mlt:n} native Egyptian listeners confirms the dialect finding and rates \BA as Egyptian as its base model (no significant difference)
(\cref{sec:dissociation,sec:results}).

\paragraph{The name}
In ancient Egyptian belief, the \emph{ba} is the part of a person's soul that carries their personality and
individuality; it is usually drawn as a bird with a human head \citep{taylor2001death}. A voice is among the most
personal things a person has, and \BA exists to let people speak, in their own voice, in their own dialect. The Arabic
letter \ar{beh} is read \emph{ba}; the project's mark draws it with a voice wave.
