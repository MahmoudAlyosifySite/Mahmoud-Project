\begin{abstract}
World models are tested for physical understanding by their surprise: an impossible event should
raise their prediction error. A rare but lawful event raises it too. We add that missing control:
a benchmark of possible, improbable and impossible billiards clips in four families, where the
improbable clips are as unusual as the impossible ones but break no law. On ideal latents, every
reading of prediction error ranks lawful fast clips above clips whose energy jumps (AUROC
\AEnergyPemax{} for the largest error, \AEnergyPerel{} for the best reading). The largest error of the
own predictor of a pixel JEPA we trained does the same (\Cp{EnergyPemaxAll}); its best reading
reaches \Cp{EnergyPerelAll}. A
label-free score assembled from known parts (slow features at long lag, read between bounces and
collisions) separates impossible from improbable clips on ideal latents where a conserved quantity is
broken (\AEnergyInv{} and \AStochasticInv{}) and is blind to a teleport, which conserves everything.
It is fragile: per-frame latent noise of 0.003 standard deviations removes the energy invariant. On the
pixel JEPA's own latents, whose linear read-out of the frame stays far below our pre-registered
gate, it finds no conserved quantity at all, and two label-free measures place that latent where the
method already fails on synthetic noise. We release the benchmark, the protocol and every run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A world model is often judged by its surprise. Shown an event that breaks a physical law, a model
that has learned the law should find it less expected than a lawful event, and with latent world
models surprise is read off prediction error
\citep{maes2026lewm,bordes2025intphys2,hristov2026surprise}. Prediction error, however, also rises
for events that are lawful but rare. A ball far faster than anything seen in training is hard to
predict and breaks no law. Existing tests pair a possible clip with an impossible one; they have no
lawful control that is just as unusual. IntPhys~2 names ``the general prediction difficulty of the
video'' as a confound of single-video scoring \citep{bordes2025intphys2}, and Hristov et al.\ find
that ordinary difficulty already raises the error on lawful clips \citep{hristov2026surprise}.

Physics separates the two cases cleanly. An impossible event changes a conserved quantity: a ball
vanishes and the count drops, a ball speeds up in mid-flight and the energy jumps. An improbable
event changes no conserved quantity, however unusual it is. This suggests a test and a tool. The
test adds a third condition, \emph{improbable}, to the usual possible and impossible clips. The
tool reads conserved quantities from a latent and asks whether one of them took a new value.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A three-way benchmark} of possible, improbable and impossible billiards clips, in
  four families of violation, each with an improbable control matched to it
  (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). The number that matters is the AUROC of impossible against
  improbable.
\item \textbf{Evidence that prediction error conflates the two} (H1). On ideal latents, every
  prediction-error reading ranks lawful fast clips above clips whose energy jumps
  (Section~\ref{sec:stageA}).
\item \textbf{A label-free invariant score, assembled from known parts}, and a map of when it
  works. On ideal latents it separates impossible from improbable where a conserved quantity is
  broken. It fails with frame-to-frame noise in the latent far smaller than a learned encoder
  produces, and with strong nonlinearity (Section~\ref{sec:stageB}). On the latents of a small
  pixel JEPA trained by us it finds nothing: no low-degree function of the latent is even
  approximately conserved, so the score is at chance by construction. We trace this to an encoder
  whose latent reads the frame poorly and moves roughly between frames, close to the level where Stage B
  already fails (Section~\ref{sec:stageC}).
\end{enumerate}
The learner itself is not new: the same criterion finds an energy-like invariant in a DreamerV3
latent \citep{bao2026invariant}, and its roots are Slow Feature Analysis \citep{wiskott2002sfa} and
Koopman theory \citep{kaiser2018conservation}. What we add is its use as a detector, the handling
of events (bounces and collisions) so that it works with contacts and several objects, and the
three-way test.

All hypotheses, thresholds and the go rule were written down before any confirmatory run
(\texttt{docs/hypotheses.md} in the repository); the two changes made since are dated there and
described in Section~\ref{sec:stageC}. All results come from one working session and have not yet
been rerun by an independent session, which our own protocol requires before a finding counts.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Surprise in world models.} Violation-of-expectation tests read surprise from prediction
error. IntPhys~2 pairs two possible with two impossible videos per scene, across permanence,
immutability, continuity and solidity, and finds predictive models near chance
\citep{bordes2025intphys2}; the full text has no lawful-but-rare condition. LeWorldModel tests
surprise on a teleport and a colour change \citep{maes2026lewm}. Hristov et al.\ ask when latent
prediction error supports surprise claims at all and find that lawful difficulty already raises it
\citep{hristov2026surprise}; their study is diagnostic and proposes no alternative score. For
language models, the distinction between impossible and merely improbable events is studied
directly, and models draw it poorly \citep{kauf2023event,michaelov2025sherlock}.

\paragraph{Conservation inside learned world models.} Bao corrects a learned invariant of a
DreamerV3 latent to stabilise rollouts on a pendulum, with the same long-lag criterion we use, and
names contacts, several interacting objects and actions as the open case
\citep{bao2026invariant}. Wang et al.\ and Wang use conservation for stability, counterfactuals and
certified horizons \citep{wang2026conservation,wang2026certified}; Noether Networks learn
conserved quantities as a meta-learned loss \citep{alet2021noether}. None of these, in what we
read, uses an invariant to detect a violation. Liao and Cao show that an invariant can be linearly
decodable from a dynamics model at $R^2 \approx 1$ and still have no causal effect on its next-state
prediction \citep{liao2026objective}. Our score reads the representation; it makes no claim about
what the model uses.

\paragraph{Roots of the learner.} Slow Feature Analysis finds the slowest-varying functions of a
signal \citep{wiskott2002sfa}. Kaiser et al.\ find conserved quantities as the null space of a
Koopman-generator regression on a function library and use them as coordinates for control
\citep{kaiser2018conservation}. In process monitoring, Shang et al.\ use slow features to tell a
normal change of operating point from a fault in the dynamics \citep{shang2015concurrent}, which is
the same separation as ours in another field. We could not obtain the full text of that paper and
describe it only from its abstract-level summary.

\paragraph{When latents are linear in the state.} Klindt et al.\ give conditions under which a
LeJEPA latent is linear in the world state and report a linear read-out below $R^2 = 0.5$ when those
conditions fail on real trajectories \citep{klindt2026lejepa}. Balestriero and LeCun prove that
separately trained JEPAs agree up to a rotation under their assumptions
\citep{balestriero2026platonic}, which motivates our hypothesis H4; their analysis does not cover
action-conditioned predictors.

\section{The three-way benchmark}\label{sec:benchmark}

\paragraph{World.} Two or three identical balls of radius 0.06 move in a unit box for 120 steps, at
speeds between 0.006 and 0.014 box lengths per step, with elastic collisions. Walls are specular,
except in the \emph{stochastic} family, where every wall bounce adds a random angle (standard
deviation 12 degrees) while keeping the speed.

\paragraph{Conditions.} \emph{Possible} clips are ordinary lawful clips. \emph{Improbable} clips are
lawful and unlike training: one ball starts three times faster than any training ball, or, in the
stochastic family, one rough-wall bounce takes an angle three to four standard deviations from the
mean. \emph{Impossible} clips break one law at one step between steps 40 and 80:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}\toprule
Family & Impossible event & Conserved quantity broken\\\midrule
energy & a ball's speed is multiplied by 0.5 or 1.6 in mid-flight & energy\\
permanence & a ball vanishes & count and energy\\
stochastic & a ball gains speed (factor 1.6) at a rough wall & energy, among random bounces\\
continuity & a ball teleports & none\\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Unit tests check that every possible and improbable clip conserves energy and count to $10^{-12}$
and that every impossible clip changes exactly the intended quantity at exactly one step. The
continuity family is included on purpose: a teleport breaks no conserved quantity, so an invariant
score should be blind to it, and we report it as an expected failure.

\paragraph{Metric.} For each score and family we report the AUROC of three comparisons over 200
clips per condition; the one that matters is impossible against improbable. 0.5 is chance, and a
value below 0.5 means the score ranks lawful rare clips above impossible ones.

\section{Learning what never changes}\label{sec:method}

Let $z_t$ be the latent of frame $t$ and $u_t = (z_t,\; z_t - z_{t-1})$. Let $\phi(u)$ collect all
monomials of $u$ up to degree two (after a PCA of $u$ when the latent is large). An invariant is
$I(u) = w^\top (\phi(u) - \bar\phi)$ with $w$ solving
\begin{equation}
  \min_w \; \mathbb{E}\big[\big(w^\top(\phi(u_{t_2}) - \phi(u_{t_1}))\big)^2\big]
  \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathrm{Var}\big[w^\top \phi(u)\big] = 1,
\end{equation}
where $t_1, t_2$ are two clean steps of the same clip between 30 and 90 steps apart, and the
variance is over all clips. This is a generalised eigenproblem; its smallest eigenvalues give the
candidates. At lag one it is Slow Feature Analysis; the long lag, which spans bounces and
collisions, is what separates a conserved quantity from a merely slow one.

\paragraph{Events.} A finite difference across a bounce is not a velocity. A step is \emph{clean}
when the latent's second difference stays below a threshold (a quarter of the median step length,
raised for clips that curve everywhere). Invariants are read only on clean steps and compared
across events.

\paragraph{Selection.} A candidate is kept if its long-lag autocorrelation on held-out lawful clips
is at least 0.9.

\paragraph{Score.} For each clip, \texttt{inv} is the largest lasting shift of any kept invariant:
at every split point, the median over the clean steps in the 12 steps before is compared with the
median after, in robust units of the local spread. A spike at an event does not move a median; a
conserved quantity that takes a new value does. \texttt{inv\_rel} divides that shift by the clip's
median shift.

\paragraph{What is fitted on what.} The event threshold, the invariants and the selection use only
ordinary lawful clips. No labels, no improbable or impossible clips and no simulator state enter
the fit or any choice among settings. Ground truth (energy, count) is used only for diagnostic
$R^2$ values.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Each latent comes with a predictor: in Stages A and B a residual MLP fitted to
predict $z_{t+1}$ from $u_t$ on the same lawful clips; in Stage C the JEPA's own predictor. Three
readings of its per-step error are scored: the largest (\texttt{pe\_max}), the mean
(\texttt{pe\_mean}), and the largest relative to the clip's own 90th percentile (\texttt{pe\_rel}),
which forgives clips that are hard everywhere. For each family the best of the three is chosen after
the fact, in the baseline's favour. \texttt{oracle\_pe} is the true simulator's one-step error, a
ceiling for prediction error rather than a competitor.

\paragraph{Hypotheses and go rule (fixed in advance).} H1: the best prediction-error score has AUROC
impossible vs improbable at most 0.70. H2: \texttt{inv} beats it by at least 0.15 as a mean over
seeds and by more than 0 in every seed. The go rule asks H2 to hold in at least two of the three
conservation families (energy, permanence, stochastic). \texttt{inv} with a velocity span of one
step is the primary score; \texttt{inv\_rel}, spans of 2, 4 and 8, and PCA sizes of 8, 16 and 32 are
declared secondary.

It has no velocities; the learner must build them from differences. Seeds 1 to 5 were run.

\paragraph{The sanity conditions hold.} \texttt{oracle\_pe} reaches \AEnergyOraclepe{},
\APermanenceOraclepe{} and \AContinuityOraclepe{} impossible vs improbable in the three
deterministic families, as it must. In the continuity family \texttt{inv} is near chance against
possible clips (\AContinuityInvPoss{}) and well below chance against improbable ones
(\AContinuityInv{}): a teleport conserves everything, and the fast improbable ball shifts the
invariants more than the teleport does.

\paragraph{H1: prediction error conflates the two.} In the energy family, \texttt{pe\_max} and
\texttt{pe\_mean} give AUROCs of \AEnergyPemax{} and \AEnergyPemean{}: they rank nearly every lawful
fast clip above every clip whose energy jumps. The best reading, \texttt{pe\_rel}, reaches
\AEnergyPerel{}. In the stochastic family the best reading is \AStochasticBestpe{}. Even the true
simulator's error is no help there (\AStochasticOraclepe{}), because a rough wall makes every bounce
unpredictable. Permanence is the exception: a vanishing ball is easy for every score
(\APermanencePemax{} for \texttt{pe\_max}), so H2 cannot hold there by construction and the go rule
rests on the other two families.

\paragraph{H2 on ideal latents.} \texttt{inv} reaches \AEnergyInv{} in the energy family and
\AStochasticInv{} in the stochastic family; the go rule gives \AGo{} (\ANfam{} of 3 families). The
kept invariants explain true energy and count with $R^2$ at least \ARtwoEnergyMin{} and
\ARtwoCountMin{} in every seed and world. This is a sanity check and proves little: on these
latents energy is a degree-two polynomial by construction.

\paragraph{Sensitivity.} Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} moves the speed of the improbable ball (scale 2
and 4 instead of 3) and the gain at the rough wall (1.2 and 2.0 instead of 1.6). The verdict is GO
in every case. Two results qualify H1. A larger gain makes the stochastic violation easier for
prediction error: at gain 2.0 its best reading reaches \SensGainTwoStochasticBestpe{}, above the
0.70 ceiling of H1. A larger speed scale makes the improbable clips easier to tell apart: at scale 4
the best prediction-error reading in the energy family reaches \SensScaleFourEnergyBestpe{}. H1 is
therefore a statement about these scenario parameters, not a law.

\subsection{Stage B: where it breaks}\label{sec:stageB}

Stage B bends the ideal latent with a random nonlinearity, $z \mapsto A s + \alpha \tanh(B s)$ with
$s$ the standardised latent and $A$ a random rotation, and adds independent Gaussian noise to every
frame, in units of each dimension's spread. Seeds 1 to 3 were run on the energy and stochastic
families (Table~\ref{tab:stageB}, Figure~\ref{fig:stageB}).

\paragraph{Nonlinearity.} Without noise, an energy invariant is still found at $\alpha = 0.1$
($R^2$ \BAZeroOneNZeroSOneRtwo{}) and $\alpha = 0.3$ (\BAZeroThreeNZeroSOneRtwo{}), and \texttt{inv}
stays above the best prediction error in the energy family (\BAZeroThreeNZeroSOneInv{} against
\BAZeroThreeNZeroSOnePe{} at $\alpha = 0.3$). At $\alpha = 1$ the energy invariant is lost ($R^2$
\BAOneNZeroSOneRtwo{}) and \texttt{inv} falls to \BAOneNZeroSOneInv{}, below chance.

\paragraph{Noise.} Noise is far more damaging. With per-frame noise of 0.003 standard deviations, on
an unbent latent, the energy invariant is already lost ($R^2$ \BAZeroNZeroZeroZeroThreeSOneRtwo{})
and \texttt{inv} in the energy family is \BAZeroNZeroZeroZeroThreeSOneInv{}, below chance. A
velocity built from a difference of two noisy frames is dominated by the noise once the per-step
motion is small, and these balls move about one percent of the box per step. A longer velocity
baseline (span 4) does not rescue it (\BAZeroNZeroZeroZeroThreeSFourInv{}).

\paragraph{What a latent must be like.} For low-degree invariants to be readable, the latent must
be close to linear in the state and nearly free of frame-to-frame noise relative to the per-step
motion. This also gives a label-free check of any encoder: does its latent keep a candidate whose
long-lag autocorrelation on lawful clips is high? The kept count in Table~\ref{tab:stageB} shows the
check failing exactly where energy is lost (one fewer invariant is kept).

\subsection{Stage C: a pixel JEPA's own latents}\label{sec:stageC}

\paragraph{Model.} A small JEPA world model in the style of LeWorldModel \citep{maes2026lewm}: a
convolutional encoder maps a $48 \times 48$ grayscale frame to a vector, and an MLP predictor maps
the embeddings of frames $t-1$ and $t$ to that of frame $t+1$. One loss, trained end to end with no
stop-gradient, no EMA target and no pretrained weights: the squared prediction error plus a SIGReg
term that pushes random one-dimensional projections of the embeddings towards a standard normal. Our
SIGReg is a re-implementation; we checked it numerically against the \texttt{SIGReg} class of the
LeWorldModel repository and aligned it (1024 projections, the statistic per time step), before any
Stage C run. One model is trained per seed and world (specular, and rough for the stochastic family)
on 400 lawful clips. The invariant learner, the event detector and the scores are exactly those of
Stages A and B; the prediction-error baseline is the JEPA's own predictor.

\paragraph{The encoder gate, and a change made to it.} The protocol reads Stage C only if a linear
probe of the latent explains the frame with $R^2$ at least 0.9. The probe as first written regressed
the latent on slot-indexed ball positions. All balls look the same, so a single frame cannot say
which ball is in slot 0, and that target is not a function of the image: a linear probe from the
raw pixels themselves reaches only \DPixSlots{} on it, against \DPixCount{} for the ball count and
\DPixOcc{} for the occupancy map below. With the owner's approval,
and before any Stage C AUROC existed, the target was replaced by two functions of the frame that
do not depend on ball identity, with equal weight: the number of balls, and a $12 \times 12$
occupancy map scored as the share of its variance explained. The threshold did not change. The
change is dated in the repository's hypotheses file.

\paragraph{Tuning, on seed 0 only.} We varied the four levers the protocol allows (training steps,
regulariser weight, latent size and frame size) over \TuneN{} settings, choosing on the probe and the
collapse statistic alone. The probe ranged from \TuneProbeMin{} to \TuneProbeMax{}; no setting
collapsed (smallest per-dimension standard deviation \TuneStdMin{} or more). The gate of 0.9 was not
reached. We froze the setting with the highest probe (latent size 512, regulariser weight 1.0, 20,000
steps, 48 pixels) and ran seeds 1 to 3 once. As the protocol requires, we report their AUROCs but do
not read them as a test of H2.

\paragraph{The encoder.} On the confirmatory seeds the probe lies between \Cp{ProberTwoMin} and
\Cp{ProberTwoMax} in every seed and world (Table~\ref{tab:stageCenc}): the ball count is read with
$R^2$ between \Cp{ProberTwocountMin} and \Cp{ProberTwocountMax}, the occupancy map between
\Cp{ProberTwooccupancyMin} and \Cp{ProberTwooccupancyMax}. The old slot-indexed probe gives at most
\Cp{ProberTwoslotsMax}.

\paragraph{No invariant is found.} The selection rule keeps \Cp{KeptMax} invariants in every seed and
world, for the primary run and for each of the \CNOptions{} runs counting the declared secondary
options (spans 2, 4, 8; PCA sizes 8 and 32; Table~\ref{tab:stageCkept}). The best candidate's
long-lag slowness on held-out lawful clips is between \Cp{SlowbestMin} and \Cp{SlowbestMax}, where
the selection rule asks for at most 0.2 and an unrelated quantity scores about 2. With nothing kept,
\texttt{inv} and \texttt{inv\_rel} are constant and their AUROC is 0.5 in every family by
construction. The go rule's output is \Cp{Go} (Table~\ref{tab:stageCverdict}). This is not evidence
against H2 on a good encoder; it is evidence that this encoder exposes no conserved quantity in low
degree.

\paragraph{H1 replicates on the learned model's own prediction error.} In the energy family the
JEPA's \texttt{pe\_max} and \texttt{pe\_mean} reach \Cp{EnergyPemaxAll} and \Cp{EnergyPemeanAll}
impossible vs improbable: the model is far more surprised by a lawful fast ball than by a ball whose
energy jumps. The best reading, \texttt{pe\_rel}, reaches \Cp{EnergyPerelAll}. Permanence and
continuity are easy for prediction error (\Cp{PermanencePemaxAll} and \Cp{ContinuityPemaxAll} for
\texttt{pe\_max}). In the stochastic family the best reading, \texttt{pe\_mean}, reaches
\Cp{StochasticBestpe}, above the 0.70 of H1; there the learned model does better than the true
simulator's one-step error (\Cp{StochasticOraclepeAll}), which a random bounce defeats.

\paragraph{Why the latent hides energy.} Table~\ref{tab:diag} adds two label-free properties of the
latent and compares them with the Stage B latents where the method was seen to work or fail.
\emph{Roughness}, the median size of the latent's second difference relative to its first, is
\DRoughMin{} to \DRoughMax{} for the three encoders. The ideal latent scores \DRefIdealRough{} (it
moves in straight lines between bounces), the bent latent at $\alpha = 0.3$ that still works scores
\DRefAZeroThreeNZeroRough{}, and the noisy latent at noise 0.003, where Stage B already lost the
energy invariant, scores \DRefAZeroNZeroZeroZeroThreeRough{}. The learned latent is far
rougher than the latent that works and within a factor of two of the one that fails.
The event detector, which on the ideal latent trusts \DRefIdealClean{} of the steps and so finds the
bounces, trusts at least \DCleanMin{} of the steps of the learned latent: it no longer sees the
bounces, as with the noisy latents. A nonlinear (MLP) read-out of the frame is no better than the
linear one (occupancy at most \DMlpOccMax{} against \DLinOccMax{}; count \DMlpCountMax{} against
\DLinCountMax{}), so the frame is not merely bent in the latent: part of it is missing.

\subsection{Do two encoders agree? (H4)}\label{sec:h4}

H4 asks whether invariants found in two independently trained encoders of one world agree
(canonical correlation at least 0.9). We fitted invariants for each encoder on its own lawful clips
and compared them on one shared set of fresh lawful clips (Table~\ref{tab:agreement}).

\paragraph{Positive control.} Ideal latents passed through two independently drawn mixers (different
seeds for the mixer and the training clips) agree: the smallest canonical correlation among kept
invariants is \HCtlAZeroCcmin{} at $\alpha = 0$ and \HCtlAZeroThreeCcmin{} at $\alpha = 0.3$. The
control also exposes a weakness of H4 as stated. At $\alpha = 1$ the encoders still agree
(\HCtlAOneCcmin{}) on the \HCtlAOneKmax{} invariants they keep, but those invariants explain at most
\HCtlAOneRtwomax{} of the variance of the true energy, while they explain the ball count with $R^2$ at
least \HCtlAOneRtwocountmin{}. Agreement between encoders does not show that the shared quantity is
the one that matters; here the encoders agree on the count, which is easy, and both lose the energy.

\paragraph{Learned encoders.} The Stage C encoders keep no invariant (\Hp{Kmax} kept), so there is
nothing to compare, and H4 cannot be tested on them. The second half of H4, using agreement to filter
invariants and lower false alarms, is not implemented.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The learned encoder never passed its gate.} The main question, whether learned invariants
separate impossible from improbable events on a learned latent (H2), is left open. Our encoder
reached a probe far below the gate, and the four levers the protocol allows did not move it
further. A better encoder (spatial features, a different objective, more data, or a published model)
may behave differently; Stage B says what it would need.

\paragraph{The method is fragile.} Frame-to-frame noise of 0.003 standard deviations, on a latent that
is otherwise ideal, removes the energy invariant (Section~\ref{sec:stageB}), and a longer velocity
baseline does not help. Low-degree invariants also need a latent close to linear in the state. Our
balls move about one percent of the box per step, which makes velocities from differences
particularly sensitive; slower or faster worlds would shift the threshold, and we did not test that.

\paragraph{The ideal latent is more ideal than pixels allow.} The Stage A latent stores each ball in
a fixed slot, so it knows which ball is which. A single rendered frame does not. Stage A therefore
shows what the method can do with a latent that carries identity, which a pixel encoder of identical
balls cannot provide without tracking.

\paragraph{The gate was changed during the study.} The first probe target needed ball identity and
could not be met by any encoder; we replaced it before any Stage C AUROC was computed and kept the
threshold, with the owner's approval, and dated the change. A reader should still weigh that the gate
is not the one first written.

\paragraph{Continuity is invisible to invariants.} A teleport conserves everything; \texttt{inv} is at
chance against possible clips and below chance against improbable ones in that family. Prediction
error catches it. The two signals are complementary and a combined detector is not built here.

\paragraph{H1 depends on the scenario parameters.} With a stronger gain at the rough wall the best
prediction-error reading exceeds 0.70 on ideal latents, and on the learned model it exceeds 0.70 in
the stochastic family at the default parameters. H1 holds in the energy family in every setting we
ran and should be read as a statement about these scenarios.

\paragraph{Permanence cannot show a gain.} Every score detects a vanishing ball, so H2 cannot hold
there by construction; the go rule effectively rests on two families.

\paragraph{Scope.} One synthetic world, small models, a passive world without actions, and
dissipation-free dynamics. The worlds where published world models are trained are driven by actions
and lose energy, so they have few exact invariants; hidden per-episode constants are the candidate
there and are not tested here.

\paragraph{Unreplicated.} Every result here comes from one working session. Our protocol counts a
finding only after a second run with new seeds launched by a different session; none has been done.
The regulariser is a re-implementation checked numerically against the official code, not the
official file. One closely related paper \citep{shang2015concurrent} could not be read in full.

\section{Conclusion}

Adding a lawful but improbable condition to the usual possible and impossible clips changes what a
surprise test shows. In the energy family, every reading of prediction error from a predictor fitted
to ideal latents ranks lawful fast clips above clips whose energy jumps, and so do the largest and the
mean error of a pixel world model's own predictor; its best reading reaches only \Cp{EnergyPerelAll}. A detector that reads conserved quantities from the latent makes the distinction
on ideal latents, where a conserved quantity is broken, and is blind to continuity violations, as it
should be. It does not survive the latent of the small world model we trained: no low-degree function
of that latent is conserved, and two label-free properties, roughness between frames and the loss of
visible events, place the latent where the method was already seen to fail with synthetic noise.
Whether better encoders, and published world models, expose conservation in a readable form is the
question this benchmark is built to answer; it is the subject of the next stage.
